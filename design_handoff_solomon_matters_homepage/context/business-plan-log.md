## 2. Los Angeles as a Starting Region

**Eric:** I am currently in greater Los Angeles metro area. Is this a good region to start in?

**Claude:** Yes — strong fit, with some nuances:

- **New construction volume:** ~2,900 new housing units authorized in the LA-Long Beach-Anaheim metro in January 2026 alone; steady weekly permit issuance
- **ADU boom:** LA's accessory dwelling unit market is large and growing, with 60-day ministerial review (21–30 days with pre-approved standard plans) — ideal for the reusable, repeat-floor-plan pre-rough-in packages proposed in the plan
- **All-electric building code (2026):** New CA homes require electric-first systems (heat pumps, electric water heaters, smart panels) — a natural tie-in for automation/energy management consulting
- **Receptive, affluent buyer base:** High home values and buyers already primed for smart features
- **Competition:** LA already has an established CEDIA integrator community — full-service installation is more crowded than the advisory/design niche, reinforcing the plan's positioning strategy
- **Licensing specificity:** California's C-7 low-voltage license is triggered by a **$1,000+ billable labor/materials threshold**, not just voltage — worth confirming with the CSLB or an attorney exactly where "consulting" ends and licensable work begins

**Net:** Good, arguably better-than-average metro to start in, especially leaning into the ADU + pre-construction advisory niche.

## 3. California C-7 Low Voltage Contractor License — Difficulty

**Eric:** How hard is it to get the C-7 low voltage contractor license?

**Claude:**

- **Experience requirement (the real bottleneck):** 4 years of journey-level low-voltage experience within the prior 10 years, verified by a qualified individual. Up to 3 of the 4 years can be substituted with accredited education or a completed apprenticeship, but a minimum of 1 year of hands-on field work cannot be waived.
- **Exam difficulty:** Not the hard part — ~50% pass rate without prep, 90%+ with proper prep courses. Two exams required: trade exam (115 questions, 73% to pass) and Law & Business exam (same format).
- **Cost:** $450 application fee, $200 license fee, plus a $25,000 surety bond (typically a few hundred dollars/year in premium, not the full bond amount).
- **Timeline:** Roughly 6–9 months from serious preparation to an active license, assuming experience documentation is in order.
- **Key nuance:** If Eric stays in an advisory/design-only role (no hands-on low-voltage installation), the C-7 license may not be required at all, since the $1,000 threshold applies to billable installation labor/materials — worth confirming this boundary with CSLB or an attorney before deciding whether/when to pursue the license.

## 4. Business Name: "Solomon Matters"

**Eric:** I am thinking of the name Solomon Matters. This is a play on word between importance and the Matter smart home standard. What do you think?

**Claude:** Positive overall assessment:

- **Works on two levels:** Reads naturally as "expertise that matters" even without knowing the Matter protocol; lands as a nice wink for industry insiders who do
- **Marketing consideration:** Most clients (electricians, builders, homeowners) won't catch the pun — the name still needs to stand on its own, which it does
- **Trademark:** "Matter" is a certification mark owned by the Connectivity Standards Alliance (CSA), tied to their product certification logo. Since it's a common English word used in a surname-based pun (not claiming certification/affiliation), infringement risk looks low — but recommended a quick USPTO TESS clearance search before fully committing
- **Longevity:** Smart home standards get superseded over time (Zigbee, Z-Wave, now Matter). If the Matter protocol fades, the name still works as "Solomon Matters" = the work matters, independent of the pun
- **Domain:** Already secured (eric@solomonmatters.com)

**Verdict:** Solid choice — recommended running the trademark check before committing further (business cards, etc.).

## 5. Yelp Listing Cost & Other Low-Cost Startup Marketing

**Eric:** What is the cost to start a Yelp business posting? What are some other low cost things I can do to get started?

**Claude:**

**Yelp:** Basic business listing (claim profile, photos, hours, services, review responses) is **free**. Yelp Ads start around $5/day, with most small businesses spending $300–$500/month for real traction. Given Eric's B2B-first model (electricians/builders, not homeowners browsing Yelp), Yelp was flagged as low-priority — worth the free listing, not worth paid promotion early on.

**Other low-cost options recommended:**

- **Google Business Profile** — free, more relevant than Yelp for search visibility and referrals
- **LinkedIn presence** — fits the B2B buyer (electricians/builders) better than review sites
- **Local trade associations/meetups** — NARI, local homebuilder associations, electrician union/trade groups (~$100–$300/year); where actual client relationships form
- **CEDIA directory/lower-tier membership** — credibility signal even before full certification
- **Simple one-page website** — services, contact, case studies; buildable free/cheap on Squarespace or Wix
- **Business cards + in-person visits** to local electrical supply houses/lumber yards — low cost, high relevance
- **Nextdoor** — useful once Phase 2 (end-user consulting) begins, for homeowner referrals

**Key takeaway:** For a B2B-first model, trade-circle relationship building and Google/LinkedIn visibility outperform paid ads; save ad spend for Phase 2 when targeting homeowners directly.
